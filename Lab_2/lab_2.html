The distribution still looks like a poisson due to the fact that we're not changing the underlying distribution when we add more days. What we're doing is taking more samples, which can perhaps reduce the variance of the distribution we approximate from the data itself, but don't change the core statistics of the true distribution they originate from. The distribution starts converging to a Gaussian since the average of many independent random variables also converges to a normal distribution by the central limit theorem--if we averaged over an infinite number of days, then we would in fact have a normal distribtion as expected. This doesn't look like the Poisson distribution from B) since there, we morally only took more samples (and didn't actually change the distribution itself). Here, we are measuring an entirely new random variable, which this average of all the poisson random variables, whose distribution doesn't necessarily have to follow that of the original random variables.

The discovery of optical/infra-red counterparts of Neutron star mergers

You have an alert from LIGO, but no associated detection from SWIFT.
This could be because it is a black hole-black hole merger, a black

B) If you have to look for a signal in 10k pixels, what is the
probability distribution of your background? (Clearly state the
statistical question, then turn that into math using your background
distribution from part 1 of this problem.)
 
We are still going to be looking for the brightest spot in the picture (since if we've made a discovery, it'll have to come from the brightest spot and perhaps its surrounding pixels). So to evaluate the significance of our reading, one must look at the distribution of the maximum of all the background distributions. That is:
$$ \mathcal{B} \sim \max{(X_1, \dots, X_{10k})} \,; X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 2)$$
Which means that the brightest spot will need to be quite bright to be significant since we have 10k draws over the background Gaussian and one can expect a couple of naturally occuring background bright spots.
 
C) Taking your brightest candidate signal from the region (assume it
has the same signal as in part 1), calculate the significance of your
detection.
 
Let x be the point we draw from the entire background distribution. Note that the probability x is higher than Y is one minus the probability that the draws from all pixel background distributions are smaller than Y (where the probability a draw is smaller than Y is just the CDF at Y):
$$\mathbb{P}(\max \ge Y) = 1 - \mathbb{P}(x \le Y)^{10k}$$

The significance of this signal is 0.99947 and our sigma for this reading on all pixels is: -3.27
 

Problem 4



The statistical issue we were exploring in the previous problem is
called a trials factor (sometimes known as a look-elsewhere effect).
This is an important effect, as if you search through a million
locations, you would expect to see approximately 1 one-in-a-million
event. However, it is also often over estimated how big an impact this
makes on the sensitivity of a search. So in this part of the lab we are
going to invert the problem.

Let us again assume we have a Gaussian background (same parameters as
Problem 3).

A) Calculate the signal required for a 5-sigma detection in Version 1
In [5]:
# Calculate the survival probability corresponding to 5-sigmas and invert

signal = norm.isf(prob_Y, scale=X)
print(f"The requisite signal for a 5-sigma detection is: {round(signal, 2)}")
The requisite signal for a 5-sigma detection is: 10.0
 
B) Calculate the signal required for a 5-sigma detection in Version 2
In [12]:
# We can use the analytic form from before
prob_less_t_Y_pow = 1 - prob_Y
prob_less_t_Y = prob_less_t_Y_pow ** (1/10000)
signal = norm.ppf(prob_less_t_Y, scale=X)
print(f"The requisite signal for a 5-sigma detection is: {round(signal, 2)}")
The requisite signal for a 5-sigma detection is: 13.1
 
C) Discuss how much brighter the signal must be for discovery if you
have a trials factor of 10k. Looking at your probability distributions,
explain why the sensitivity penalty due to a trials factor is so low.
 
Based on what we calculated, a trials factor of 10k gives us a signal (for discovery) of 13.1, while for the 1 pixel case it is 10. This is an increase by about a factor of 1.31, which is not as a high compared to the 4 orders of magnitude increase in the trials factor. This is because of the exponentially decaying tails of the Gaussian. A 5-sigma event is pretty deep into the tail of the Gaussian and a small step to the right corresponds to a huge change in probability (due to the parabolic nature of the log-scale graph). This ends up meaning that the 5-sigma threshold varies logarithmically with the associated probability, which in turn varies inversely with the trials factor (if we approximate (1-p)^N appropriately)--leading to a logarithmic relationship between the trials factor and the 5-sigma threshold, just as we observe empirically.
 
D) If you changed the trials factor significantly (orders of
magnitude), how large is the effect on your 5-sigma sensitivity
threshold?
 
As stated above, it'll roughly be logarithmic in the trials factor. One can see this empirically as follows:
In [17]:
# trials factor = 10^scale
scale = 8
prob_Y = norm.sf(5)
thresholds = [0 for i in range(scale + 1)]
scales = [10**i for i in range(scale + 1)]
for i in range(len(scales)):
    prob_less_t_Y_pow = 1 - prob_Y
    prob_less_t_Y = prob_less_t_Y_pow ** (1/scales[i])
    thresholds[i] = norm.ppf(prob_less_t_Y, scale=X)

plt.semilogx(scales, thresholds)
plt.xlabel('Trials factor')
plt.ylabel('Signal needed for 5σ')
plt.show()
